# 12. Tags: faceted closed-vocabulary classification on the Abstract

This document explains the `content_tags` and `format_tags` fields on
`Abstract` — what they are, why they're shaped as two closed-vocabulary
facets rather than one open list, and how they propagate up the tree.

## Goal

A user browsing or querying their library wants to slice by *what
something is about* (math, history, law, …) and *what kind of artifact
it is* (article, book, report, …) — two questions a librarian has been
answering for a century. The Abstract already carries free-text
`topics` and `domains` fields, but those are open: useful for
embedding-based similarity, useless as filter facets, and they explode
at internal nodes when you try to roll them up.

`content_tags` and `format_tags` are designed exactly to be rolled up:
each is a list drawn from a fixed closed vocabulary (see
`service/tags.py`), and an internal node's tags are the deterministic
`sorted(set(...))` union of its children's tags. The closed vocabulary
is the bound that keeps the union from exploding at the root.

## Why two facets, why closed

The library-science precedents for this shape:

- **Ranganathan's faceted classification (Colon Classification, 1933).**
  The foundational work. Any subject splits into independent facets —
  Personality, Matter, Energy, Space, Time — that recombine flexibly.
  `content_tags` × `format_tags` is a minimal two-facet PMEST.
- **LCSH + LCGFT.** Library of Congress Subject Headings classify
  "what it's about"; the Library of Congress Genre/Form Terms (carved
  out in 2007) classify "what it *is*". The split exists because
  conflating them — e.g. one heading for "biology dictionary" — causes
  vocabulary explosion. Our `content_tags` ≈ LCSH topic, our
  `format_tags` ≈ LCGFT.
- **FAST (Faceted Application of Subject Terminology, OCLC, 2002–).**
  The modern faceted simplification of LCSH; applied across tens of
  millions of bibliographic records. Empirical evidence that closed,
  small facet vocabularies outperform open tagging for retrieval.
- **BISAC subject codes.** Used by every major publisher for ~50 years.
  Closed list. Lesson from BISAC: when publishers were allowed to add
  freeform subjects, classification quality collapsed.

Folksonomies (open Web 2.0 tagging — Flickr, del.icio.us) lost to
faceted classification for the exact reason we cite here: leaf-level
expressiveness comes at the cost of useless aggregation one level up.

## Why two separate fields instead of a composed `domain-format` string

We considered `"math-article"`, `"finance-report"`, etc. as a single
list. Two separate fields wins on three counts:

1. **Bounded growth.** With |D|=23 and |F|=13, the cartesian product is
   299 possible composed tags — and the union at the root in the
   composed scheme could carry all of them. With separated facets, the
   worst case at the root is |D| + |F| = 36 tags total.
2. **Independent JSONB facet filters.** Retrieval can filter on
   `content_tags @> ["math"]` alone, or `format_tags @> ["book"]`
   alone, without indexing every possible combination.
3. **No hyphen-parse ambiguity.** Multi-word vocabulary items
   (`earth-science`, `computer-science`) would make `math-article` vs
   `computer-science-article` ambiguous to a parser; with separated
   fields validation is a set-membership check on each list.

The downside, accepted: at internal nodes you know the subtree contains
`math` and `article` in `content_tags`/`format_tags` respectively, but
not whether the same blob carried both. That's fine — retrieval is the
consumer, and a retrieval query that wants "math articles" filters on
the leaf blobs (which still have the original pairing).

## The vocabularies

Both live in `service/tags.py` as `typing.Literal` aliases
(`ContentTag`, `FormatTag`) with the matching `frozenset[str]`
constants derived from them via `typing.get_args`. The Literal alias
is the source of truth so the JSON schema handed to the tag-classifier
LLM carries the vocabularies as `enum` constraints — out-of-vocab
strings are structurally rejected at the schema level, not just by a
Python-side validator. Current state:

- `ContentTag` / `CONTENT_TAGS` (23 items): subject-matter facet
  drawn from BISAC top-level + LCSH main classes, trimmed for a
  knowledge-worker library. STEM, social sciences, humanities, plus a
  few cross-cutting buckets.
- `FormatTag` / `FORMAT_TAGS` (13 items): genre/form facet drawn from
  LCGFT, trimmed to artifacts a personal collection actually contains.

Both use lowercase-kebab-case. There is no `other` / `general` escape
hatch — the absence forces a vocabulary PR when the lists need to grow.
This is the BISAC lesson translated to our scale.

## How to grow the lists

1. Open a PR adding the new item to the relevant `frozenset` in
   `service/tags.py`.
2. Consider whether the new item meaningfully splits a bucket or just
   duplicates an existing one. Closed vocabularies only stay useful
   while small — a 200-item `content_tags` defeats the bounded-union
   guarantee.
3. No DB migration is needed. The new tag becomes producible by the
   blob extractor on its next iteration; existing rows keep their old
   tags and will pick up the new one only on re-extraction.
4. If you want existing rows to use the new tag, delete the relevant
   `data_blobs` rows (cascading via the immutable-pipeline mechanics
   described in `docs/04.immutable_data_pipeline.md`) — the next
   extractor pass will reproduce them.

## How tags are produced

The minimum-1-per-facet constraint is a system-wide invariant: every
persisted `Abstract` — blob-level or node-level — carries at least
one `content_tag` and at least one `format_tag`. The validator on the
`Abstract` model (`service/abstract.py`) enforces this unconditionally;
the `AbstractCore` superclass (used as the LLM-direct output type on
both worker paths) carries no tag fields at all, so the constraint
only kicks in at the final assembly boundary.

The model hierarchy in `service/abstract.py`:

```
AbstractCore           — synthesized fields, no tags. LLM output type
  ├─ Abstract            for the node extractor; superclass of
  │     └─ RollingAbstract   everything that does carry tags.
  └─ RollingAbstractCore — AbstractCore + running_summary. Main blob
                            agent's output type (no tags in schema).

BlobTags               — standalone, just the two Literal-typed tag
                          fields with min_length=1. Tag-classifier
                          agent's output type.
```

- **Blob extractor** uses two LLM agents per chunk, run serially:
  - The **main agent** (`service/blob_extractor/abstract.py`)
    synthesizes the prose-bearing and entity fields and produces a
    `RollingAbstractCore`. Tags are not in its schema, so it never
    has to reason about them.
  - The **tag agent** (`service/blob_extractor/tagging.py`) is a
    narrow single-task classifier that takes the main agent's title,
    summary, topics, and domains (~200 input tokens) and outputs a
    `BlobTags`. The schema carries `enum` (via Literal) and
    `minItems: 1` (via `min_length=1`), so the LLM cannot legally
    emit an empty list or an out-of-vocab string. Per-facet soft caps
    live in `AbstractSettings.content_tags_max_count` /
    `format_tags_max_count` (default 3) and surface via the
    `tag_budgets_text` helper.
  - `process_file` then assembles a `RollingAbstract` from the core +
    tags via `RollingAbstract.model_validate(...)`, which re-runs the
    `Abstract` validator with the full constraint.
- **Node extractor** uses a single LLM agent producing
  `AbstractCore` (no tag fields). `extract_node_abstract` constructs
  the final `Abstract` from the core plus the sorted set-union of the
  children's tags; the construction re-runs the `Abstract` validator,
  so if children somehow lack tags (legacy data, bug upstream),
  construction raises and the worker's backoff loop surfaces it.

The schema-level enforcement on `BlobTags` (and on `RollingAbstract`'s
redeclared tag fields) is the primary fix for what was previously
unreliable LLM compliance with prose-only "REQUIRED" instructions.
The Python-side validators on `Abstract` (membership +
sort-and-dedupe) remain as a belt-and-braces second line of defence,
mostly relevant on the node side where the inputs come from
JSON-decoded children rather than a validated LLM output.

Legacy blobs/nodes produced before this design landed may have empty
`content_tags` / `format_tags` (or a now-removed `content_type` key)
in their JSONB. They're not re-validated at read (we never
deserialize `data_blobs.abstract` / `data_node_abstracts.abstract`
back through Pydantic on the hot path), so they coexist quietly — but
they will break the `extract_node_abstract` construction of any
ancestor that hasn't yet been computed. The fix is to drop the
affected rows; the workers' cascading invalidation triggers handle
the rest, and the next extractor pass repopulates them under the new
design.

## Why we embed the tags

Per `docs/05.blob_extractor.md`, pure-label fields like the entity
buckets are deliberately kept out of `serialize_abstract_for_embed` —
they're filter material, not similarity material. Tags break that
pattern: they ARE included in the embedding stream.

The reason: shared tags pull tag-similar blobs together during
`tree_builder`'s descent. That tightens the children of each internal
node toward a coherent facet, which in turn makes the
`sorted(set(...))` union of children's tags converge to a small set
rather than spanning the full vocabulary by accident. Worst case if the
embedding doesn't help is redundancy with the open `topics` / `domains`
fields — cheap.

## What's intentionally out of scope (for now)

- **Retrieval-side facet filtering.** The natural follow-up is a
  retrieval endpoint that accepts `content_tags` / `format_tags`
  predicates and translates them to JSONB GIN queries. Not in this
  iteration.
- **Per-facet weighting in the embedding.** Both tag lists flow into
  the abstract stream with equal weight. A retrieval-time tuning knob
  could weight them differently, but that's a downstream concern.
- **Hierarchical facets.** No nesting (e.g. `math.algebra` under
  `math`). Personal libraries at this scale don't justify the extra
  vocabulary management.
- **Auto-bootstrap of new tags from corpus statistics.** Vocabulary
  growth is a deliberate, reviewed action.
